Welcome to Physics 212, an intensive and comprehensive exploration into the fundamental principles of electricity and magnetism. Throughout this course, students will investigate the physical laws governing electric charges, electric fields, magnetic fields, electromagnetic induction, and Maxwell's equations. This course is designed primarily for engineering and physical science majors who require a rigorous mathematical foundation in calculus-based physics. By the end of the semester, you will develop strong analytical and problem-solving skills that allow you to model complex electromagnetic systems and understand modern technological applications ranging from simple circuits to advanced electromagnetic devices.

The study of electromagnetism represents one of the crowning achievements of nineteenth-century physics, unifying electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theoretical framework. In this course, we emphasize both conceptual understanding and quantitative problem-solving. Physics is not merely a collection of formulas to memorize, but a coherent language used to describe the fundamental forces shaping our universe. Students are strongly encouraged to engage actively during lectures, participate in collaborative problem-solving exercises, and connect theoretical concepts to real-world engineering challenges. Your success will depend heavily on consistent effort, active curiosity, and a willingness to tackle challenging conceptual problems.

Your instructor for this semester is Dr. Alexander Vance, Professor of Physics. Office hours will be held every Tuesday and Thursday afternoon following class, as well as by appointment in Room 402 of the Science and Engineering Building. You may also reach out via email for quick logistical questions, though conceptual physics questions are best addressed during office hours or via the course discussion board where peers can benefit from the discussion. Dr. Vance has dedicated over fifteen years to teaching undergraduate physics and researching electromagnetic properties of novel materials. He is deeply committed to helping every student succeed and master the rigorous material presented in this course.

By the conclusion of Physics 212, students will achieve several critical learning outcomes. First, you will be able to apply Coulomb's Law and Gauss's Law to calculate electric fields generated by various charge distributions. Second, you will master the concepts of electric potential, capacitance, current, resistance, and direct-current circuit analysis using Kirchhoff's rules. Third, you will explore magnetic fields, the Lorentz force, Ampere's Law, and the Biot-Savart Law to understand how currents generate magnetic forces. Fourth, you will investigate Faraday's Law of induction, alternating current circuits, and electromagnetic waves. These outcomes will prepare you for advanced coursework in engineering and applied physics.

Beyond content mastery, this course seeks to cultivate essential scientific competencies. Students will enhance their mathematical modeling capabilities by utilizing vector calculus and differential equations within physical contexts. You will also develop rigorous dimensional analysis habits and learn how to estimate physical quantities order-of-magnitude style. Furthermore, collaborative group problem-solving sessions will foster effective technical communication skills. Engineering solutions rarely happen in isolation; hence, learning how to articulate physical reasoning to peers is an invaluable professional skill that we will actively practice and assess throughout the semester.

Your final course grade will be determined through a weighted combination of homework assignments, quizzes, a mid-semester examination, and a cumulative final examination. Weekly homework assignments account for twenty percent of your final grade. Regular online and in-class quizzes will contribute fifteen percent. The mid-semester examination administered during week eight will count for thirty percent, while the comprehensive final examination during week sixteen will represent the remaining thirty-five percent of your total grade. Letter grades will be assigned based on standard university grading scales, with absolute percentage thresholds outlined in the course syllabus document.

Final letter grades follow a standard ten-point scale where ninety percent and above earns an A, eighty percent and above earns a B, seventy percent and above earns a C, sixty percent and above earns a D, and anything below sixty percent results in an F. The instructor reserves the right to apply a curve to overall course grades if examination performance warrants it, though a curve will only ever benefit students and never lower their calculated letter grade. Extra credit assignments are not offered under any circumstances, so students must focus on performing consistently across all regular assessments, homework assignments, and major examinations throughout the semester.

To reinforce classroom learning, there will be exactly one major homework assignment due every week. When completing your weekly homework assignments, simply writing down a final numerical answer is never sufficient for receiving full credit. You must clearly demonstrate all intermediate steps, algebraic manipulations, and fundamental physical principles utilized to arrive at your solution. Start every problem by stating the known variables, drawing a clear diagram where appropriate, and citing the governing physical laws such as Gauss's Law or Faraday's Law. Developing this disciplined, methodical approach to problem-solving is essential not only for succeeding on examinations in this course, but also for tackling complex engineering design problems in your future professional careers.

During Week 1 of the semester, we will introduce electric charge, the fundamental properties of matter, and Coulomb's Law, establishing our foundational mathematical framework without any homework due per syllabus policy. In Week 2, our focus shifts entirely toward the concept of electric fields, continuous charge distributions, and the behavior of point charges in uniform electric fields. Students will practice calculating electric field vectors using integration techniques. Weekly homework assignment number one will be due on Thursday of Week 2 right before class at 12:00 PM, marking the commencement of regular weekly assessment submissions.

Week 3 introduces Gauss's Law, exploring electric flux and symmetric charge distributions including infinite sheets, lines, and spherical shells. Weekly assignment number two is due on Thursday before class. Moving into Week 4, we examine electric potential energy, electric potential, and potential gradients associated with various charge configurations. Students will learn how potential fields relate directly to conservative electric forces. Homework assignment number three will be due on Thursday of Week 4 right before class at 12:00 PM, continuing our steady progression through core electrostatic principles.

Week 5 investigates capacitance, dielectrics, and energy storage in electric fields, analyzing parallel-plate, cylindrical, and spherical capacitors. Homework assignment number four is due on Thursday. In Week 6, we transition to current, resistance, and electromotive force, examining Ohm's Law and microscopic models of electrical conduction within solid conductors. Students will calculate current densities and power dissipation in various resistive networks. Homework assignment number five will be due on Thursday of Week 6 right before class at 12:00 PM, preparing students for upcoming circuit analysis modules.

Week 7 covers direct-current circuits, applying Kirchhoff's loop and junction rules to analyze complex multi-loop resistor networks, RC circuits, and transient charging behavior. Homework assignment number six is due on Thursday. Week 8 marks our major mid-semester examination covering all electrostatics and DC circuit topics. The exam occurs during our regular Thursday class time slot at 12:00 PM in Lecture Hall 210, with no separate homework assignment due this week as students focus their efforts on exam preparation and comprehensive review.

Following the mid-term examination, Week 9 introduces magnetism, exploring magnetic fields, magnetic forces on moving charges, the Lorentz force law, and the motion of charged particles in uniform magnetic fields. Homework assignment number seven is due on Thursday. In Week 10, we study magnetic fields produced by currents, utilizing the Biot-Savart Law and Ampere's Law to calculate magnetic fields generated by long straight wires, current loops, and solenoids. Homework assignment number eight will be due on Thursday of Week 10 right before class at 12:00 PM.

Week 11 investigates magnetic forces on current-carrying conductors, torques on current loops, and the operation of galvanometers and electric motors. Homework assignment number nine is due on Thursday. Moving into Week 12, we explore electromagnetic induction, focusing on Faraday's Law of induction, Lenz's Law, and induced electric fields within changing magnetic environments. Students will solve quantitative problems involving motional electromotive force and generator mechanics. Homework assignment number ten will be due on Thursday of Week 12 right before class at 12:00 PM.

Week 13 covers inductance, self-inductance, mutual inductance, and magnetic energy storage, along with RL and LC circuit transients. Homework assignment number eleven is due on Thursday. In Week 14, we examine electromagnetic oscillations and alternating-current circuits, incorporating phasors, root-mean-square values, reactances, impedance, and resonance phenomena in RLC series circuits. Homework assignment number twelve will be due on Thursday of Week 14 right before class at 12:00 PM, concluding our weekly homework submission cycle for the semester.

Week 15 synthesizes our semester studies by introducing Maxwell's equations in integral and differential forms, displacement current, and the prediction and propagation of electromagnetic waves. Per course policy, no homework assignment is due during this final instructional week. Finally, Week 16 is dedicated to the comprehensive cumulative final examination. The final exam takes place during our standard class time slot and location, covering all sixteen weeks of physics coursework to evaluate your mastery of electricity and magnetism before concluding the semester.

Although the primary lecture meets twice weekly on Tuesdays and Thursdays, students registered for Physics 212 are also co-enrolled in a mandatory weekly laboratory and discussion section. These hands-on laboratory sessions provide an invaluable opportunity to observe theoretical concepts—such as electrostatic shielding, capacitor charging, and magnetic induction—manifest in real physical experiments. Laboratory reports emphasize rigorous data analysis, error propagation, and scientific writing. Active participation and completion of all laboratory experiments are mandatory prerequisites for passing the course, regardless of your lecture and homework grades.

Success in a calculus-based physics course requires disciplined time management and effective study habits.
